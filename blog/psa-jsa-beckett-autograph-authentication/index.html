PSA vs JSA vs Beckett: Autograph Authentication Explained

What the three main authenticators do, what their paperwork means and when to use them.

Published November 17, 2026 · Autograph Identifier

If you buy or sell autographs you will keep meeting three names: PSA/DNA, JSA and Beckett. They are third-party authenticators. They have no stake in the sale; they examine a signature and give an opinion on whether it is genuine. In today's market that opinion often decides whether an item sells at all.

The three main services

PSA/DNA

PSA is best known for grading trading cards. PSA/DNA is its autograph authentication division, launched in 1998. It authenticates signatures across sports, entertainment and history, and can also grade the quality of an autograph on a 1 to 10 scale.

JSA (James Spence Authentication)

JSA was founded in 2005 by James Spence Jr. and focuses on autograph authentication. It is widely seen at card shows, where collectors can submit items in person.

Beckett Authentication Services (BAS)

Beckett, long known for price guides and card grading, launched Beckett Authentication Services in 2016. Its certification is accepted alongside PSA/DNA and JSA by the major marketplaces and auction houses.

All three are broadly accepted. Some collectors and auction houses prefer one or another for particular signers, and for a few specialist areas, such as The Beatles or astronauts, dedicated experts carry additional weight.

Sticker, card or letter?

  • Sticker and certificate card: a tamper-evident numbered sticker goes on the item, with a matching card. This is the basic level, used for most modern autographs.
  • Letter of authenticity (LOA): a full-page letter describing and picturing the item, normally issued for higher-value and vintage signatures. Buyers of expensive pieces expect a full letter.
  • Encapsulation: small flat items such as cards, cut signatures and index cards can be sealed in a tamper-evident holder with the certification printed on the label.
  • Witnessed or in-the-presence certification: a company representative watched the item being signed. This is the strongest form, because it does not rely on handwriting comparison.

How to verify a certificate

Every certificate has a number. Enter it in the verification page on the authenticator's own website and check that the item described is the one in front of you. Fake stickers and stickers moved from one item to another both exist, so this step matters as much as the sticker itself.

What it costs

Fees are set per signer and rise with the value of the signature: a common modern athlete costs far less to certify than Babe Ruth. Prices change, so check the current fee schedule before you submit. As a rule of thumb, certification is worth it when the fee is small compared with the value it adds on resale.

What about manufacturer authentication?

Some athletes sign only through one company. Michael Jordan signs for Upper Deck, and Kobe Bryant signed for Upper Deck and later Panini. Those items carry the manufacturer's own numbered hologram, verified with that company, and do not need a second opinion.

Before you pay for certification

Authentication fees are usually charged whether the item passes or fails. It makes sense to screen items first.
