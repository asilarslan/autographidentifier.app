Autopen, Secretarial, Stamped and Preprint Signatures: What They Are

Four kinds of signature that are not forgeries but are not autographs either.

Published November 17, 2026 · Autograph Identifier

Collectors tend to think in two categories, real and fake. In practice there is a large middle ground: signatures that were produced legitimately, often by the celebrity's own office, but not by the celebrity's hand. They turn up constantly in inherited collections and online listings.

Autopen

An Autopen is a machine that holds a real pen and traces a stored signature pattern. Presidents, members of Congress, astronauts and busy public figures have used them for decades to answer mail.

How to recognise it: the line has the same thickness and darkness from start to finish, because a machine does not vary its pressure. Strokes often begin and end with a small dot. The decisive test is comparison: two signatures from the same pattern match exactly when overlaid, which never happens with handwriting.

Secretarial

A secretarial signature is written by an assistant, family member or employee on the celebrity's behalf. Hollywood studios did this routinely for fan mail, road managers signed for bands, and baseball clubhouse attendants signed team balls for players.

How to recognise it: it is real handwriting, so the ink tests do not help. You have to compare letter shapes with known genuine examples. Secretarial signatures are often neater and more legible than the real thing, because the writer was copying carefully.

Stamped

A rubber stamp of the signature, used on souvenir baseballs, photos and correspondence.

How to recognise it: the ink is uniform and slightly mottled, sometimes with a faint outline around the edges of the strokes. There is no variation where lines cross, and identical copies exist.

Preprint (facsimile)

The signature is part of the printed image. It was on the original photo or artwork and has been reproduced along with it. Promotional photos, trading cards and fan club items often have them.

How to recognise it: tilt the item under a light. A printed signature has exactly the same gloss as the rest of the picture and sits flat in the surface. Under magnification it is made of printing dots.

Are they worth anything?

Usually a small fraction of a genuine autograph. A vintage studio-signed Disney fan card or a clubhouse team ball still has some value as memorabilia, and an autopen letter from a president has modest value as a document. The problem arises when they are described and priced as genuine autographs.

A quick way to triage a collection

If you have a stack of signed photos to sort, check each one under a light for print, then photograph the promising ones.
